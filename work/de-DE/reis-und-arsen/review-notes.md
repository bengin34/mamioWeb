# Review notes — de-DE / reis-und-arsen (`beikost-reis-arsen`)

DRAFT. Editorial byline + DE_MEDICAL_DISCLAIMER, no clinician review.
1,141 words, 3 sources, 20 mapped claims.

## Tone decision
This topic invites alarm: inorganic arsenic is classified as carcinogenic to humans, no safe intake
could be derived, and its presence is "in jeder Menge unerwünscht" [1]. The article carries all
three of those verbatim — and also the sentence from the same assessment that an acute health
effect is unlikely for every population group in Germany at the measured levels [1]. Both halves
are needed: without the second, a parent who has been giving rice cakes reads the page as a verdict
on something already done. The last FAQ answers exactly that question and points to variety from
now on, which is what the source actually recommends.

The operative words are quoted rather than paraphrased: *nicht ausschließlich*, *in Maßen*,
*variieren* [1]. The page nowhere says rice or rice cakes are forbidden, because the BfR does not.

## Rice drinks are a different claim
For rice-based drinks the BfR reports that national and international bodies reject them for
infants — *not only* because of arsenic but *especially* because the nutrient composition does not
meet infants' needs [1]. The article keeps that emphasis, so the page does not imply a rice drink
would be fine if the arsenic were lower.

## Data age — please note
The BfR page carries the date **22.12.2020** and the measurements it reports come from the **2014**
federal/state food monitoring [1]. The EU maximum levels it describes took effect 01.01.2016 [1].
The article names all three dates and closes by telling the reader to check whether the BfR has
published newer data. Before this page is refreshed, someone should look for a more recent BfR
statement or monitoring round.

## No numbers, no cooking instructions
No mg/kg limit is given: the categories covered by the EU limits are quoted, the values were not
readable from an opened source. On preparation, the source says only that arsenic compounds can
pass partly into the water when washing and cooking rice in plenty of low-arsenic water [1] — no
ratio, no time, no "pour the water away" step. The article says the source gives the direction but
not an instruction, and adds that this route does not apply to rice-flake porridge at all.

## Dropped
mg/kg values · a cooking method with ratios · wholegrain vs. white rice for infants (no statement in
an opened source — the FAQ says so) · any "rice cakes are dangerous" framing.
